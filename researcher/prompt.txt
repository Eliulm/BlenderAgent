THE REQUIRED PART LIST IS THE MOST IMPORTANT OUTPUT. It decides which physical
parts exist in the final asset; the builder models exactly this list, so a
missing part is missing from the dataset and an unnecessary one wastes effort.
There is no fixed recipe: we rely on your engineering judgment, informed by
everything you found and your own knowledge, to decide what matters for this
object. First understand the product: how it is built, how it works, which
parts hold it together and where its conductive and dielectric material sits.
Then select parts by two criteria:

1. Structural completeness. Include every major part that makes the object
   physically hold together and function as an assembly: housings and shells,
   frames, chassis, handles, heads, lids, hinges, axles, large brackets and
   main internal carriers. If the listed parts were built for real, the
   object should not unexplained gaps. Bigger generally means more important.
   Do not list screws, clips, washers, small springs, cable ties,
   adhesive or similar minor hardware.
2. Electromagnetic relevance in 0.1–15 GHz. Include every exterior part and
   material region, since what is visible from outside dominates scattering.
   Inside, include parts that change the response even if they are small or
   structurally minor: conductive bodies and their continuous lengths (boards,
   coils and windings, cells, motors, wires and cable runs, shields, heat
   sinks, metal frames), large dielectric volumes, and apertures or slots of
   several millimetres or more. Weigh each part against its surroundings: a
   small metal hinge in otherwise plastic glasses matters a lot, while one more
   small bracket among many metal parts of a graphics card does not. A copper
   winding inside a mostly plastic object can matter; the same winding inside
   a large metal assembly may not.

Shielding: parts fully enclosed by a closed, continuous conductive shell
(a sealed metal housing without significant openings, slots or non-metal
windows) are electromagnetically invisible from outside; do not list them
unless they are needed structurally. Model the shell itself, with any real
openings. When a metal enclosure has substantial openings, plastic panels or
gaps, the internals behind them matter again.

Do not use a blanket minimum part size: a small conductive feature isolated in
dielectric may still matter, while a large but shielded part may not.
For each required part, give its material family, location/relationship to other
parts, why it matters (structural, electromagnetic, or both),
the evidence image IDs or source URLs, and whether its form is observed or
inferred. Do not invent fine hidden detail. The final list should be sufficient
to tell a builder exactly what it must include without overloading it.
Keep distinct material regions and major hidden components in distinct list
entries. Do not collapse a cell, actuator, conductive winding, circuit board,
and support frame into one generic "internals" requirement when those parts
are all relevant.

Find sourced measurements of the WHOLE product: length/width/height/depth or

Use sources that describe this exact model, not a similar revision.
After a few credible searches with no exact-model dimensions, record a scale

If the product's real size would not fit a 300 mm diameter sphere, say so in
